How rogue waves form

Tip: in 2D, the white line is the literal sum of the blue and violet lines at every point.

Superposition

The ocean surface obeys (to first order) a linear wave equation, so overlapping wave systems simply add. At every horizontal position:

η(x,y,t) = η_A(x,y,t) + η_B(x,y,t)

Where crests of A and B coincide, the surface elevations reinforce — constructive interference. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel. The 2D view shows a single slice through the sea; the 3D view shows the whole surface.

Linear focusing

A real storm swell is not one sine wave but a band of components with slightly different frequencies. Here each train bundles 7 such components. Most of the time their phases are scrambled and the sea looks moderate — the energy is spread out.

Occasionally the components drift into step at one point in space: their amplitudes add coherently and a single towering crest appears, then vanishes as the phases drift apart again. That transient pile-up of otherwise ordinary wave energy is linear (dispersive) focusing — the leading explanation for many deep-water rogue waves.

Crossing seas — why the 3D view matters

The direction sliders point each train along its own heading. When the two systems cross at an angle, their crest lines intersect at a single moving point — and only there do the two systems fully reinforce. In the 3D view you can watch that intersection sweep across the sea and occasionally erupt.

Crossing wind-sea and swell is a documented real-world rogue mechanism: analyses of the Draupner wave (25.6 m in a 12 m sea, 1 January 1995 — the first rogue ever instrumentally recorded) suggest two wave systems crossing at a large angle.

Phase alignment between trains

The two trains travel at different speeds, so train A's wave group continually laps train B's. When both groups — and the individual crests inside them — line up at the same spot, the surface can briefly reach nearly the sum of everything: that is the flash-highlighted convergence point.

Set the two frequencies nearly equal and you will see slow beats instead: broad regions of reinforcement and cancellation drifting across the sea.

Significant wave height Hs

Oceanographers characterize a sea state by the significant wave height, four times the standard deviation of the surface elevation:

H_s = 4·σ,   σ² = Σ aᵢ²⁄2

Hs is computed live from the component amplitudes of both trains. A wave qualifies as rogue when its crest-to-trough height exceeds twice the significant height:

H_max > 2·H_s

In 2D, dashed guide lines mark elevations of ±Hs∕2 and ±Hs. In 3D, any crest climbing above the Hs elevation glows orange — rogue territory.

Why rogues are rare

Two pure sine waves can never exceed 2·Hs — the statistics forbid it. It takes many components conspiring: with 14 components here, perfect alignment is possible but improbable, which is exactly why real rogue waves are rare, sudden, and were dismissed as sailors' legend until the Draupner measurement.

Recipe to hunt one: keep the two amplitudes similar, keep a healthy speed difference between the trains, and let the simulation run. Watch the Hmax∕Hs meter approach the red tick.
